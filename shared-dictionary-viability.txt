Prompt:

  I'm interested in the possibility of improving compression ratio of our links with zstd shared dictionary compression.
  We should start by doing some testing on the viability of this, without baking it into the actual code yet.
  Here's where I'd like to start. Let's download/scrape something like 10 wikipedia pages per language, of e.g. 100 most spoken languages, or
  something like that. Then train a zstd dictionary from it.
  Then generate a sample english text file, and compress it a) with zstd max compression ratio and b) with our dictionary.
  See if that provides any benefits.
  We can do it here on the cli of this machine, no need to bake it into our JS lib yet.

Results:

• The experiment shows a real but modest benefit from a shared zstd dictionary.

  Levels 19 and maximum --ultra -22 produced identical sizes for these small payloads. For the 32 KiB sample, the dictionary improves the ratio
  from 2.2905 to 2.3215 and saves approximately 255 base64url characters.

  Corpus details:

  - 100 Wikipedia language editions attempted; 99 succeeded.
  - 923 manifest-listed article introductions.
  - 775,450 bytes of training data.
  - Independent 32 KiB English holdout assembled from different articles.
  - 65,536-byte fastCover dictionary.
  - Dictionary-compressed frames were successfully verified by zstd.
  - No repository files were changed.

  My take: this establishes viability, but not yet a compelling production win. The dictionary costs 64 KiB once in the application—not per link—
  but adds versioning and decoder coupling. The next useful experiment would train on actual Linkify tar payloads/Readability HTML and benchmark
  hundreds of held-out captures. That should be substantially more representative and may exploit recurring tar headers, HTML structure,
  attributes, and metadata better than plain Wikipedia prose.
